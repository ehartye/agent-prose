# Reference excerpts and how to use them

These are bounded reference passages, not a training corpus or an automatic leveling key.
Keep three evidence types distinct: text actually assessed in a study, standards-selected exemplars,
and original demonstrations. A standards band does not certify every sentence or every reader.

## An actual assessed passage: explained technical terminology

> Primary hypersomnias, which are conditions that make people very sleepy, are not common.

This is an assessed model output in Table 4 of Abdullah Barayan, Jose Camacho-Collados and Fernando
Alva-Manchego, [*Analysing Zero-Shot Readability-Controlled Sentence Simplification*](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.20246v2),
COLING 2025. Human raters assigned B1 and a classifier C1. The example illustrates the limits of a
metric that penalizes an explained specialist term. It is an English-L2 CEFR assessment example,
not a U.S. grade mapping, a student-learning result or a medical teaching model.

Reuse: the linked version is [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/).
The excerpt is unchanged. Keep the authors, title, source and licence with it; identify any revision.
Do not delete essential vocabulary just to imitate a lower classifier label.

## Standards-selected texts across the requested school years

[Common Core Appendix B](https://tools.achievethecore.org/content/upload/ELA.Literacy%20Appendix%20B.pdf)
selects works and proposes reading tasks using complexity judgments. Its permissions for copyrighted
selections do not grant a general republication licence. The short passages below have separate
public-domain-in-the-USA source records. Attribution identifies the underlying authors; the source
transcriptions are linked for inspection. Preserve their wording when quoting.

### Grades 4–5: viewpoint and connected sentences

> Alice was beginning to get very tired of sitting by her sister on the bank, and of having nothing to do…

Lewis Carroll, *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland*, chapter I (1865).
[Text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/11/11-h/11-h.htm),
[public-domain record](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/11).
Appendix B pp. 63 and 70 selects the work and an illustration/text relationship task (RL.4.7).
Use it to examine viewpoint and how clauses connect. The ellipsis marks truncation; an adaptation
should preserve who is bored and why, rather than merely shortening the sentence.

### Grades 6–8: agency and historical context

> With their kindly aid, obtained at different times and in different places, I finally succeeded in learning to read.

Frederick Douglass, *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave*, chapter VII (1845).
[Text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/23/23-h/23-h.htm),
[public-domain record](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/23).
Appendix B pp. 91 and 93 pairs the work with an objective-summary/central-ideas task (RI.8.2).
Use an explanatory frame to supply historical context and resolve pronouns. Do not erase the
author's agency, circumstances or historical meaning when unpacking syntax.

### Grades 9–10: plain wording with substantial knowledge demands

> We are met on a great battlefield of that war.

Abraham Lincoln, *Gettysburg Address* (1863).
[Text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/4/4-h/4-h.htm),
[public-domain record](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/4).
Appendix B p. 123 places the speech in this band. Ask what “that war” refers to and how this
sentence serves the speech's argument. Its simple local wording does not establish the demand
of the whole work. A freshman lesson should supply needed context while retaining the source.

### Grades 11–college/career readiness: explain an argument without flattening it

> I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately, to front only the essential facts of life…

Henry David Thoreau, *Walden*, “Where I Lived, and What I Lived For” (1854).
[Text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-h/205-h.htm),
[public-domain record](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/205).
Appendix B pp. 167 and 171 uses the work for interacting central ideas (RI.11–12.2).
Explain what the argument asks the reader to consider, then return to the original language.
The ellipsis marks truncation. An accessible paraphrase should preserve the ideas and qualifications.

## Using the examples responsibly

Use the reference for a specific decision: explain a term, supply context, unpack a relationship,
or preserve an argument. Adaptations you write are new demonstrations, not the study's tested text.
Label them as such and review fidelity. For informational teaching, also consult the source-backed
teaching guide's worked-example and explanatory-question guidance; literary excerpts do not validate
a science-textbook template. None of these short samples is large enough to certify a grade score.
