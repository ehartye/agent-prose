# Assessment of source-1.md

**Judgment:** Promising content for grade 9, but it needs targeted revision before an average freshman can reliably read it independently. The chief obstacle is the explanation of research, especially in P1 and P2. Understanding the percentages does not by itself prepare readers to understand the causal limitation.

Ratings describe the passage for the specified reader: **Ready** = sufficient as written; **Revise** = a localized barrier; **Major support** = likely to prevent the intended understanding without explanation. These are editorial judgments, not measured student performance.

| Criterion | Rating | Evidence and implication |
|---|---|---|
| Sentence processing | Revise | P1 places the duration, setting, two groups, two percentages, and a causal qualification in one sentence. Readers must retain the comparison while waiting for the main conclusion. |
| Vocabulary | Revise | P2 defines self-report clearly. Association is essential but undefined; “construed,” “prior attainment,” “held constant,” and “attributed” add avoidable difficulty. |
| Required background | Major support | Readers have not studied research design. P2 names unassigned sleep schedules and uncontrolled factors but does not make their relevance concrete enough for independent reading. |
| Numerical comparison | Revise | The comparison is mathematically accessible, but the different denominators are embedded in P1. Both sleep and homework completion are reported behaviors. |
| Organization and reasoning | Ready, with a local revision | The sequence moves from finding to limitations to interpretation. P3 usefully rejects both “sleep caused completion” and “sleep has no effect.” A clearer bridge from P2 to P3 would strengthen it. |

## Proposed changes

1. **Divide P1 by function.** Introduce the six-week survey at one school; present the two groups and their results; then state the causal limitation. As a local replacement for the comparison, consider: “Among students who reported at least eight hours of sleep, 68 percent also reported finishing their homework. Among students who reported less sleep, 45 percent reported finishing their homework.” Keep the duration and setting nearby. Do not present either percentage as a share of all surveyed students or as independently measured completion.
2. **Teach the essential term where it first appears.** Explain association as a pattern linking reported sleep and reported homework completion in these responses. Retain the word association so students learn it. Replace “should not be construed as evidence” with “does not show” or “does not establish.”
3. **Keep self-report and its definition in P2.** The existing explanation is helpful. If an additional caution is needed, explain that students' descriptions are not the same as direct measurements; do not imply that students necessarily lied or misremembered.
4. **Make the research limitation explicit in ordinary language.** Replace “prior attainment” with “earlier school achievement” and “obligations outside school” with “responsibilities outside school.” Explain that the researchers did not make sure the groups were similar on these factors, so the survey cannot separate their possible influence from sleep's possible influence. Do not assert that a particular factor actually caused the difference.
5. **Preserve P3's two-sided qualification.** Keep both points: this survey alone cannot establish a causal effect, and it does not prove that sleep has no effect. A brief embedded check could ask readers to explain that distinction using the survey's limitations.

The revision should retain the six-week duration, one-school setting, association, self-report, 68 percent, 45 percent, and the causal limitation. Success means a reader can identify the observed pattern and explain why it does not establish the cause, without needing a separate lesson in research terminology. No full replacement passage is proposed here.

The CLI's Flesch–Kincaid estimate was 9.4. That surface estimate is broadly consistent with the intended school grade, but it does not establish that a reader without research-design instruction will understand the reasoning. The targeted revisions above address that gap.
