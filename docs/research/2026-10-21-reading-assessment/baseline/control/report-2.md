# Assessment of source-2.md

**Judgment:** Suitable for a grade 7 teacher-led literature lesson with focused discussion supports. Its uncertainty is productive: students can distinguish what the narrator observes, what the narrator infers, and what the narrator later doubts. Making every event explicit would weaken the assigned task.

Ratings describe suitability for this lesson: **Strong** = serves the task well; **Support useful** = a manageable obstacle with teacher support; **Revise** = an obstacle that should be changed before use. These are editorial judgments, not tested comprehension results.

| Criterion | Rating | Evidence and implication |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence for inference | Strong | The unmoved cup and latched window complicate Mara's explanation that the wind took the envelope. Students have concrete details to cite without being given a verdict. |
| Viewpoint and limits of knowledge | Strong | “Perhaps I only remember a smile because of what happened later” explicitly questions the narrator's recollection. A first-person report gives access to this narrator's account, not direct access to Mara's thoughts. |
| Productive uncertainty | Strong | The passage withholds what happened later and who removed the envelope. Several interpretations remain possible; that uncertainty supports evidence-based argument. |
| Syntax and cohesion | Support useful | P1's cup-location sentence carries a condition about who might know the hiding place. The window clause adds a habitual detail. These sentences reward rereading and are not automatically defects because they are complex. |
| Vocabulary and time references | Support useful | “Latched” may need a quick gloss. “At twelve” and the shift to “what happened later” invite questions about time. The bright road provides a clue for the time of day without settling every chronological question. |
| Independent resolution | Support useful | Students may assume there must be a single proven culprit. Teacher framing should make clear that the objective is to justify an inference and state its limits. |

## Suggested supports and restrained changes

- **Use an observation–inference–limit chart.** For example: the narrator reports that the window was latched and the cup had not moved; this gives a reason to question the wind explanation; it does not prove who took the envelope. Treat even observations as details reported by this narrator rather than independently verified facts.
- **Contrast certainty in the wording.** Pair “the window ... was latched” with “or perhaps I only remember a smile.” Ask what makes the second claim less secure. The smile cannot serve as unqualified proof of Mara's guilt.
- **Read the dialogue against the narrator's actions.** “I told her it did not matter” is evidence of what the narrator said. Not asking Mara to help search may suggest distrust, hurt, secrecy, or another motive. Students should explain their reading rather than equate the spoken statement with certain knowledge of the narrator's feelings.
- **Provide a short vocabulary gloss and a second reading.** Explain latched as fastened shut. Have students track the envelope, cup, window, and shelf across P1 and P2. Avoid a preliminary explanation of who took the envelope or why the cup was placed there.
- **Use prompts that preserve the inference work.** Ask: “What does the narrator have grounds to doubt, and which details support that doubt?” and “What can the narrator not know from the evidence given?” Accept multiple interpretations when their evidence and limits are stated.

No sentence simplification is necessary before this teacher-led use. If “At twelve” consistently distracts students from viewpoint analysis, consider “At noon” only after confirming that this is the intended meaning. That would remove incidental ambiguity while leaving the envelope's disappearance, the narrator's memory, and Mara's motives unresolved.

A successful response would cite a detail, explain the inference it supports, and identify something it does not establish. Naming Mara as the definite culprit is not warranted by the passage alone.

The CLI's Flesch–Kincaid estimate was 3.6. That low surface estimate does not make the inference task trivial: it does not measure the demands of retrospective narration, uncertain memory, or distinguishing suspicion from knowledge. The passage should be assessed as a literary scene for the stated lesson.
