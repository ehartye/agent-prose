# What does “given” change?

Conditional probability answers a probability question when we already know that a condition is true. The goal is to identify which group to count. A familiar fraction can then express the answer.

A fictional club has 100 members. Of these, 40 play a musical instrument, 60 play a sport, and 30 do both. Suppose we select one member at random, with every member equally likely to be chosen.

Recall that a fraction’s denominator, the bottom number, tells how many members are in the group being counted. Its numerator, the top number, counts those who also meet the requirement in the question.

First ask: **Given that the member plays a sport, what is the probability they play an instrument?** “Given” limits us to the 60 sports players. Among them, 30 play an instrument. The probability is 30/60 = 1/2.

Now reverse the condition: **Given that the member plays an instrument, what is the probability they play a sport?** We now count only the 40 instrument players. Of these, 30 play a sport, so the probability is 30/40 = 3/4.

Both fractions count the same 30 members on top. Their denominators differ because their conditions allow different groups.

With no condition, the probability that a randomly selected member does **both** is 30/100 = 3/10. Here, the denominator includes the whole club.

The key question is: “Which members does the condition let us count?” These proportions describe this fictional club. They do not show that playing a sport causes someone to play an instrument, or the reverse.

## Practice

Given that a member plays a sport, what is the probability they do **not** play an instrument?

## Answer key

Of the 60 sports players, 60 − 30 = 30 do not play an instrument. The probability is 30/60 = 1/2.
