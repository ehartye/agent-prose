# Conditional probability: choosing the right group

A probability compares the outcomes you want with all the outcomes you could get. Conditional probability does the same thing, but with a smaller group. A condition tells you which outcomes to consider. The word **given** introduces that condition.

Imagine selecting one member at random from a fictional club of 100 members. Each member has the same chance of being selected. Of these members, 40 play an instrument, 60 play a sport, and 30 do both.

Without a condition, all 100 members are possible choices. The probability that the selected member does both is therefore 30/100, or 3/10. This is the probability of the **overlap**: belonging to both groups.

Now suppose you learn that the selected member plays a sport. What is the probability that this member also plays an instrument?

“Given that the member plays a sport” limits your attention to the 60 sports players. Of those 60 members, 30 play an instrument. The probability is 30/60 = 1/2. The denominator is 60 because the condition has changed the group being counted.

Reverse the question: given that the member plays an instrument, what is the probability that this member also plays a sport? Now consider only the 40 instrument players. The probability is 30/40 = 3/4.

All three fractions count the same 30 members in the numerator. Their denominators differ: 100 for the whole club, 60 for sports players, and 40 for instrument players. Reversing a condition can change the answer. These counts do not show that either activity causes the other.

## Practice

Given that the selected member plays a sport, what is the probability that they do **not** play an instrument?

---

## Answer

Of the 60 sports players, 30 do not play an instrument: 60 − 30 = 30. The probability is 30/60 = 1/2.
