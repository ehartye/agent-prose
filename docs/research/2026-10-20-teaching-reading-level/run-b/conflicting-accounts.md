## For a sixth grader

Two eyewitnesses can describe the same event differently without either person lying. They may have watched from different places or at different times. Their reasons for telling the story may differ, and their memories may be incomplete. Even an account written when the event happened can contain mistakes.

To judge conflicting accounts, historians use **corroboration**: checking an account against other, independent accounts. Independent accounts do not simply copy one another. Five accounts repeating one person’s story do not give five separate pieces of evidence.

Ask what each person could have seen, when they wrote, and why. Look for independent support for each claim. If a disagreement cannot be settled, explain what remains uncertain.

## For a high school senior

Conflicting eyewitness accounts do not necessarily mean that someone lied. Differences in vantage point, timing, purpose, and memory can produce different descriptions of the same event. An account written during or soon after an event may still be inaccurate; being contemporary does not guarantee reliability.

**Corroboration** tests claims by comparing independent accounts. Before treating agreement as supporting evidence, ask whether the accounts arose independently or copied a common source. Repetition through copying does not supply independent confirmation.

Interpret each account in light of what its author could observe, the time between the event and the account, and the purpose of recording it. Then assess which claims have independent support. Where contradictions remain unresolved, preserve the uncertainty rather than force a single, fully settled account.
