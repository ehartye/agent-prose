An **observation** is something you notice or measure about weather happening now or something that happened earlier. Looking outside and seeing rain is an observation. Reading yesterday’s temperature from a thermometer record also gives you an observation. You are using evidence about weather that has already happened.

A **prediction** tells what you think will happen later. “It will rain tomorrow afternoon” is a prediction. Tomorrow’s rain has not happened yet, so no one has observed it.

Weather forecasters connect these two ideas. They collect observations, such as temperature, wind speed, air pressure, and where clouds are moving. They also use computer models. A model uses information and rules about how weather works to calculate what might happen next.

Forecasts are useful for making plans, but they are not promises. Weather can change in ways that are hard to predict. Forecasters may miss a storm’s path or the time rain begins. Even a forecast based on careful observations can be wrong.

You can still use a forecast to make a sensible plan. If rain is likely, take a raincoat. Later, compare the prediction with observations of what actually happened. That comparison helps show where the forecast worked and where it missed.
