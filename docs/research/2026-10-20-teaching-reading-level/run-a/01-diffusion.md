# Diffusion: random motion, predictable change

How can particles moving randomly produce movement in a particular direction? By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain that connection and predict how a concentration gradient affects diffusion.

Particles in fluids, including liquids and gases, move randomly. Each particle can change direction; it does not choose a destination or seek an empty space. To understand the overall result, we need to consider many particles together.

**Concentration** describes how much of a substance is present in a given volume. A **concentration gradient** is a change in concentration across a distance. When concentration differs between two connected regions, particles can move between them in both directions.

Consider this invented illustration: a container of liquid has more coloring particles per unit volume on its left side than on its right. There is no barrier between the sides. Because more coloring particles start on the left, more tend to cross toward the right than cross toward the left during the same time. Both movements happen, but they do not balance.

**Net movement** means the balance of movement after opposite directions are taken into account. **Diffusion** is net movement down a concentration gradient, from higher concentration toward lower concentration, resulting from random particle motion. No particle needs to know which side has a lower concentration. The difference emerges from the movements of many particles.

As the coloring spreads, its concentration becomes more even. Eventually, the liquid can reach **dynamic equilibrium**: particles keep moving, but movement in opposite directions balances over time. There is no net flow due to diffusion. Equilibrium does not mean that particles have stopped.

## What changes the rate?

Across the same distance, a larger concentration difference makes a steeper gradient. Under otherwise identical conditions, a steeper gradient increases the net diffusion rate. Temperature, the distance particles travel, and the medium they move through also affect the rate. Comparing gradients alone requires keeping these other conditions the same.

For example, imagine two containers with the same liquid, temperature, and distance between regions. The container with the steeper gradient will have the greater net diffusion rate. If their temperatures also differ, the gradient alone is not enough to explain the comparison.

## Try it

1. At dynamic equilibrium, do particles stop? Explain.
2. Two otherwise identical containers have different concentration gradients. Which has the greater net diffusion rate, and why must the other conditions match?

## Answer key

1. No. Particles continue moving randomly; opposing movements balance over time, leaving no net diffusion.
2. The steeper gradient gives the greater net rate. Matching other conditions isolates the gradient's effect.

Random motion continues throughout diffusion. The gradient determines the direction of net movement, not the path of each particle.
