# Interpreting conflicting historical accounts

Conflicting eyewitness accounts do not by themselves establish that someone lied. Witnesses may have occupied different vantage points, observed different moments, written for different purposes, or remembered details differently. A **contemporaneous** account comes from the time of the event, but proximity in time does not guarantee accuracy.

**Corroboration** involves comparing independent accounts to assess where they support or challenge one another. Independence matters: several accounts copied from a single report repeat that report rather than supply separate evidence. Apparent agreement therefore requires examining how the accounts are related, as well as what they say.

Interpret a disagreement by asking what each witness could have observed, when the account was produced, and how its purpose and the witness's memory may have shaped it. Compare the claims with independent accounts. The goal is to explain which conclusions the available evidence supports and where it remains limited. Unresolved contradictions should remain visible as uncertainty, rather than disappear through an unsupported choice between accounts.

This fictional exercise supplies no actual historical sources or quotations. It supports a method for interpreting disagreement, not a verdict about particular witnesses or events.
