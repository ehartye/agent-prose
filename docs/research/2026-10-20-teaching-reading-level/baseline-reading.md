# High school freshman rewrite

Students in this study who said they slept at least eight hours on school nights also reported higher average grades than those who slept less. The link remained after researchers accounted for age and school. However, they did not measure family resources, existing health conditions, or study habits, which could help explain the difference.

The study does not show that getting more sleep causes better grades. Its findings apply to the participating schools and may not hold for all teens. A study that randomly assigns students to a sleep program could test cause and effect, but researchers would still need to consider how students are assigned, whether they follow the program, and how results are measured.

# Fourth grade explanation

Imagine you look out the window and see rain falling. You have made an observation. An observation tells what someone sees or measures now, or what someone saw or measured earlier. You could also use a thermometer to observe that the air is 50 degrees.

A prediction tells what someone thinks will happen before it has been observed. If you say, “It will rain tomorrow,” you are making a prediction. Tomorrow’s rain has not happened yet, so you cannot observe it today.

Weather experts make predictions called forecasts. They gather observations about clouds, wind, temperature, and other weather conditions. Then they use computer models to help figure out what may happen next. These models use rules about how weather works.

A forecast is useful, but it is not a promise. The weather can change in ways experts did not expect. A forecast might say rain is likely, yet your neighborhood could stay dry.

You can still use that forecast to decide whether to bring an umbrella. Later, you can compare the prediction with observations of what actually happened. Remember: an observation records weather that has already been seen or measured. A prediction describes weather that has not been observed yet.
